Josephine Connor to Monica Pizura Lineage Research

Connor Family Origins and the Farm at Connor’s Creek

The Connor family’s roots in Detroit trace back to the late 18th century. Josephine’s grandfather, Henry “White Hair” Connor (1780–1840), was among the area’s early settlers. As an infant, Henry arrived in Michigan with Moravian missionaries and Christian Indian converts in 1782. Growing up fluent in native languages, Henry became an Indian interpreter and sub-agent, assisting Governor Lewis Cass during frontier treaty negotiations.

In the early 1800s, Henry Connor acquired a 640-acre ribbon farm along the Detroit River, on the northeast outskirts of Detroit. Through this tract flowed a small stream originally known as Tremble’s Creek (after French settler Joseph Tremblay). After Henry gained possession of the land—by marrying Teresa Tremblay, according to local tradition—the waterway came to be called Connor’s Creek in his honor. Henry’s farm, situated near the present intersection of Jefferson Avenue and Conner Avenue, became the homestead of the Connor family for generations. By 1832, the Connor’s Creek area had grown into a village, later annexed by the City of Detroit in 1917.

Henry Connor’s influence extended beyond his farm. He served on Michigan’s Territorial Legislative Council in the 1820s and was elected township supervisor in 1818, reflecting the Connors’ early role in local governance. When Henry died in 1840, he was remembered as a prominent pioneer, farmer, and interpreter for the region. His legacy lived on not only in the name of Connor’s Creek but also in the large family that continued to occupy and develop the surrounding lands. Even in the late 19th century, observers noted that Henry’s descendants “occupy adjacent lands” along Connor’s Creek. This Connor homestead at Connor’s Creek and Jefferson became part of Detroit’s urban landscape, a tangible reminder of the family’s deep roots in the area.

Richard H. Connor: Public Service and Family Leadership

Josephine’s father, Richard Henry Connor (1814–1903), carried forward the family’s prominence. Born on July 26, 1814, on the Connor homestead in Grosse Pointe Township, Richard grew up on his father’s farm along Connor’s Creek. In adulthood, he emerged as a respected civic figure, serving as township supervisor and justice of the peace in Grosse Pointe. In 1849, during a cholera scare in Lansing, he was sergeant-at-arms of the Michigan House of Representatives—famously tasked with rounding up legislators who had fled the state capitol in fear of the “brain fever” epidemic.

Richard H. Connor’s political career peaked when he was elected as a Michigan state representative from Wayne County for the 1859–60 session. A loyal Democrat like his father, he was known for his steady leadership and commitment to public duty.

Richard’s first marriage was to Basilide “Bosalique” St. Aubin, a member of the prominent St. Aubin family (early French settlers for whom St. Aubin Street is named). After Basilide died young (after bearing three children), Richard remarried in 1849 to Catherine “Archange” Rivard, widow of Charles Rivard and herself from another old French-Canadian family. Together, Richard and Catherine had eight more children, bringing the combined household to 16 children including those from Catherine’s first marriage.

This large Connor brood included Mary Josephine (Josephine) Connor (born May 1852) and several siblings: William J. Connor (born c.1854), Augustus P. Connor (born 1857), Delia S. Connor (married Gilbert Moran), and Ida L. Connor (married into the Beaubien family). William managed portions of the ancestral farm until his death in 1894, while Augustus pursued business interests. The family’s alliances linked the Connors to other influential French-Michican families.

Richard H. Connor lived to see the 20th century, passing away in 1903 and being laid to rest in Detroit’s Mt. Elliott Cemetery. He is remembered as an Indian trader, farmer, and public official, with his farm noted as once being at Jefferson and Beaubien in old downtown Detroit. The Connor family’s name had by then become synonymous with the area’s history.

Josephine Connor Lancashire: Society, Family, and Community

Mary “Josephine” Connor Lancashire (1852–1921) was born into this storied Detroit family in May 1852, likely at the Connor homestead or nearby Grosse Pointe Township. On February 17, 1879, she married Lawrence Henry Lancashire (1851–1895), an English-born businessman who had settled in Detroit. This union represented the blending of two prominent lineages: Josephine brought her French-Moravian Detroit heritage, and Lawrence brought the ambition of a rising mercantile family.

Lawrence Lancashire had emigrated from England as a young man and quickly made his mark in Detroit’s business community, becoming vice president of Mabley & Co. department store. The couple settled in the city’s First Ward, enjoying an “excellent social environment” and every advantage that came with their status.

Josephine’s contributions were largely in the social and charitable spheres typical of women of her standing in that era. She was active in church and community events, hosted gatherings, and lent her support to charitable causes. Josephine and Lawrence had at least four children—Forrest Henry Lancashire (1879–1949), Herbert W. Lancashire, Marie L. Lancashire (married Joseph W. Keenan), and Lucy Hazel Lancashire (married Ralph S. Bird). Through these children, Josephine’s lineage extended into other respected families and even across the country.

In her later years, Josephine remained a respected figure in Detroit society, witnessing the transformation of the Connor family’s old farmlands and the city’s growth. She died on April 5, 1921, at the age of 68, and was laid to rest likely in the family plot. Her passing marked the loss of another link to Detroit’s pioneer past.

Integration of the Connor and Lancashire Families

The marriage of Josephine Connor and Lawrence Lancashire symbolized the fusion of old Detroit aristocracy with the new entrepreneurial class. Josephine’s side included generations of early Detroit settlers—French habitants, Moravian missionaries, and territorial leaders—while Lawrence’s side brought the energy of an immigrant family that achieved success in America’s Gilded Age.

The Lancashire-Connor household became a hub of social activity, bridging English, Irish, and French-American circles in the city. Their children’s marriages extended this network further, reinforcing both social and economic capital. Josephine’s active role in Detroit’s social charities and clubs helped preserve the Connor legacy even as Detroit modernized.

Josephine’s life bridged the frontier Detroit of her grandfather and the industrial Detroit of the early 20th century. The Connor family remained a part of the public consciousness, contributing historical continuity and civic identity, while the Lancashire side contributed to Detroit’s commercial development.

Legacy in Michigan’s Development and Civic Life

The Connor family’s contributions to Michigan’s development are woven into the fabric of Detroit and its environs. Connor’s Creek provided water power for early mills and later became the site of city infrastructure, including the Conners Creek Power Plant built in 1915. Multiple generations of Connors served in public roles, shaping governance and the economy of southeast Michigan.

Through marriage alliances, the Connors bolstered other endeavors, tying into early French-Canadian contributions and notable Detroit families. These extended familial networks fostered cooperation in community projects and charitable works, such as church founding.

By the time Josephine Connor Lancashire passed away in 1921, Detroit had transformed, but the Connor name remained on Detroit’s map and in its histories, underscoring the family’s foundational status in the region.

In summary, Josephine Connor Lancashire’s life and legacy reflect a convergence of Detroit heritage, helping to establish early farms, local government, and even place-names, while the Lancashire connection contributed entrepreneurial energy and commercial growth. Their marriage bound these threads together, and Josephine’s own contributions included community service and the preservation of family ties and traditions.

Catherine “Archange” Rivard (1818–1893): Life and Legacy

Early Life in Alsace (Bas-Rhin, France)

Catherine C. Bauer, later known as Catherine “Archange” Rivard, was born on January 27, 1818 in Strasbourg, Alsace. She was born into a French-speaking Catholic family, and her cultural background blended French and German heritage. Her Catholic faith and French heritage played a pivotal role in her life and integration into North American settler society.

Emigration to North America

In the 1830s, Catherine left Alsace and ventured to North America, likely settling among French-speaking Catholics in Michigan. She arrived in southeastern Michigan by 1837, where a longstanding French-Canadian community lived.

Marriage to Charles Rivard (1837) – Integration into French Detroit

On January 16, 1837, Catherine married Charles Rivard in Detroit. Charles was a member of an established French family of Grosse Pointe. Catherine became known as “Archange” within the community. They had five children, baptized likely at Ste. Anne’s Church or the local parish in Grosse Pointe.

Catherine lived on or near the Rivard family farm at Grosse Pointe. After Charles’s death in 1848, Catherine was left a young widow with five children.

Second Marriage to Richard H. Connor (1849) – A Blended Family

In 1849, Catherine remarried Richard Henry Connor, combining two sets of children into one household. Catherine and Richard had eight more children together, making a blended family of sixteen children. Catherine managed the household, raised the children, and instilled values of faith and education.

French-Canadian and Alsatian Cultural Roots

Catherine’s life exemplified the blending of Alsatian-European and French-Canadian cultures in 19th-century Michigan. She remained a devout Roman Catholic, and her heritage was part of the Connor family legacy in Detroit.

Later Life, Legacy, and the Connor Family in Detroit

Catherine saw Detroit transform from a small frontier town to a major industrializing city. Her most significant contributions to the Connor legacy were through her children’s accomplishments and family lines, especially her daughter Josephine Connor.

Catherine died on December 22, 1893, at the family home in Grosse Pointe Township. She was laid to rest at Mt. Elliott Cemetery in Detroit, remembered as the beloved matriarch of one of Detroit’s pioneer families.

Conclusion

Catherine “Archange” Rivard’s journey from Alsace to Detroit is a testament to resilience and adaptability. She brought rich traditions of French Alsatian Catholic culture, reinforcing Detroit’s Francophone heritage. Through two marriages, Catherine became the cornerstone of a sprawling family, and her legacy lives on in the Connor family name and descendants.

Lineage from Forrest Lancashire to Monica Pizura

Forrest Henry Lancashire (1879–1949) was born in Detroit, Michigan, to parents Lawrence Henry Lancashire and Mary Josephine (Connor) Lancashire. He married Lois Harding in 1906, daughter of Frank W. Harding and Nannie L. (Davenport) Harding, linking the Lancashire line with the Hardings.

Forrest and Lois relocated to California, living in Woodlake, Tulare County. Forrest died in Tulare County in 1949 and was buried in Woodlake District Cemetery.

They had two daughters: Lois Justine Lancashire (1911, died in infancy) and Justine Lois Lancashire (1912–1992). Justine married James Edward Robinson in 1937, and they lived in Tulare County, California.

Justine and James Robinson had at least one daughter, Monica, born around 1939–1940. Monica Robinson later married John “Jack” Pizura, and together they have been long based in Woodlake, California, at the historic ranch property. Monica is now the innkeeper of the Wicky-Up Ranch Bed & Breakfast, representing the fourth generation of her family to occupy and maintain that home.

Through Monica, the line of descent is clear:

  • Lawrence H. Lancashire (1851–1895) m. Josephine M. Connor (1852–1921) → their son…
  • Forrest Henry Lancashire (1879–1949) m. Lois Harding (1885–1954) → their daughter…
  • Justine Lois Lancashire (1912–1992) m. James E. Robinson (1912–1981) → their daughter…
  • Monica (Robinson) Pizura (b. ~1940), fourth-generation owner/operator of the family’s Woodlake ranch.

Each generational step is supported by vital records and credible sources, ensuring a reliable chain from Forrest Lancashire down to Monica Pizura.

Conclusion

Forrest Henry Lancashire married Lois Harding in 1906, and together they had a daughter Justine. Justine married James Robinson and had a daughter Monica, who by marriage became Monica Pizura. Monica Pizura, now in her mid-80s and living in Woodlake, California, is Forrest Lancashire’s granddaughter. The familial chain—Lancashire to Harding to Robinson to Pizura—is well-documented through genealogical records, census data, and the homestead that has passed down through the generations.
